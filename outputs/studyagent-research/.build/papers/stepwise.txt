INTRODUCTION
Problem-solving is central to mathematics education. Successfully
solving a problem requires students not only to know mathematical
concepts, but also to interpret the problem situation, identify rele-
vant knowledge, and organize and carry out a solution. Accordingly,
mathematics educators regard students’ problem-solving processes
as important instructional targets [3, 17, 35].
Diagnosing students’ performance at each stage of the problem-
solving process is essential for supporting their learning [21, 29].
Yet in practice, it remains heavily dependent on teachers’ profes-
sional judgment, grounded in their close examination of students’
problem-solving behavior or written work [15, 51, 59]. Since stu-
dents do not consistently externalize their reasoning without ex-
plicit support [12, 54], prior work has emphasized designing tasks
that intentionally elicit students’ thinking [32, 35], such as written
explanation prompts. In parallel, research on mathematical prob-
lem solving has proposed stage-based frameworks that decompose
the solving process into structured phases [30, 49, 52, 58], offering
conceptual guidance on what kinds of reasoning may occur at each
step. However, it remains unclear how these general stages can
be translated into problem-specific questions that elicit diagnosti-
cally informative responses. It also remains unclear how teachers
can consistently assess those responses within everyday classroom
workflows.
Motivated by this gap, we conducted a formative study with four
mathematics teachers (mean teaching experience: 13.25 years) and
48 students in grades 3–6. Using four general stage-based questions
adapted from prior work [30], we examined how teachers diag-
nosed students’ problem-solving processes and what challenges
they encountered. We identified two challenges. First, questions
that were not tailored to individual problems often failed to elicit
the reasoning needed to solve them. Second, teachers needed clear,
problem-specific rubrics to assess students’ reasoning consistently,
but constructing such rubrics demanded substantial time and peda-
gogical effort. These challenges pointed to the need for a framework
that specifies both what reasoning to elicit and what evidence to
assess at each stage.
To address this need, we developed STEP (STage-based Evalua-
tion of Problem-solving skills), a diagnostic framework co-designed
with six mathematics education experts and informed by established
problem-solving frameworks [24, 34, 52, 58, 62]. STEP organizes
problem solving into four sequential stages and refines each stage
into two diagnostic sub-skills. These sub-skills distinguish sources
of difficulty within a broad stage—for example, identifying key
information versus identifying the problem goal, or applying math-
ematical knowledge versus constructing a mathematical expression.
By specifying what evidence to elicit and assess for each sub-skill,
STEP helps locate where students’ reasoning begins to break down.
Building on STEP, we developed an LLM-based pipeline that
instantiates this diagnostic structure for individual math word prob-
lems. Given a problem, the pipeline generates a sub-question tar-
geting each sub-skill and a corresponding rubric that characterizes
different levels of student reasoning. Automating this problem-
specific generation reduces the manual effort required to create
diagnostic materials across problems, while the shared structure
supports consistent process-level assessment.
We evaluated our approach in two stages. First, eight mathemat-
ics teachers (mean teaching experience: 11.83 years) participated in
an expert review of materials generated for 36 problems spanning
grades 3–6. Six conducted the primary review, while two additional
teachers resolved disagreements. At the sub-skill level, 91.32% of the
materials met the construct-validity criterion, while 90.97% met the
mathematical-accuracy criterion. However, level appropriateness
(80.21%) and problem-context alignment (72.22%) received lower
ratings, revealing occasional mismatches with grade-level language,
curricula, and problem contexts. These limitations made teacher
review and adaptation a central design requirement. Accordingly,
we incorporated teachers’ curriculum materials into the generation
pipeline and enabled teachers to review and edit its outputs. These
refinements informed STEPwise, a teacher-in-the-loop system for
diagnostic material generation and process-level assessment.
We then conducted a month-long classroom study with 5 teach-
ers and 64 students in grades 3–9 to examine how STEP and STEP-
wise support process-level diagnosis in classroom use. Over the
month, teachers generated diagnostic materials for 90 problems in
total, yielding 720 sub-questions and corresponding rubrics. Teach-
ers reported two main benefits: they could produce diagnostic mate-
rials for their own problems easily, and the step-by-step responses
let them see how far students had progressed and pinpoint where
the problem-solving process began to break down—a level of de-
tail they could otherwise obtain only through individual follow-up
questioning. They further noted that beyond diagnosis, the sub-
questions themselves serve as a learning experience, guiding stu-
dents to work through one problem thoroughly and helping them
develop structured thinking beyond simply applying memorized
procedures. Students responded positively to the stage-based ap-
proach in terms of its usefulness for working through problems,
expressing their reasoning, recognizing their strengths and weak-
nesses, and willingness to use it for future learning (all means
between 3.6 and 3.9 out of 5; Figure 5). At the same time, the format
was not equally easy for everyone: the students who struggled
most with writing out each stage were those least accustomed to
articulating their reasoning. Teachers noted that these students are
the ones who need this kind of diagnosis and practice most, but
suggested that for them adjacent stages could be merged into fewer
sub-questions to reduce their burden.
This work makes three contributions. First, we introduce STEP,
a diagnostic framework co-designed with mathematics education
experts that refines four problem-solving stages into eight sub-
skills and specifies what evidence to elicit and assess at each stage.
Second, we present an LLM-based pipeline that generates problem-
specific sub-questions and rubrics based on STEP, and STEPwise, a
teacher-in-the-loop system that supports diagnostic material gen-
eration and process-level assessment. Third, through a formative
study, an expert review, and a month-long classroom deployment,
we provide empirical findings on the practical challenges of fine-
grained diagnosis, the strengths and limitations of LLM-generated
diagnostic materials, and how teachers and students engage with
stage-based diagnosis in classroom practice.

RELATED WORK
Our work builds on three interconnected areas: theoretical frame-
works of mathematical problem-solving stages, practical approaches
to assessing and supporting these processes in educational settings,
and recent advances in using AI for education.
2.1
Problem-Solving Process Frameworks in
Mathematics Education
Understanding and teaching problem-solving as a process has been
a central concern in mathematics education for decades. Polya’s
seminal work established a foundational four-stage model consist-
ing of understanding the problem, devising a plan, carrying out the
plan, and looking back [52], emphasizing mathematical problem-
solving as deliberate cognitive activities at each stage [52]. Studies
have shown that Polya’s framework leads to significantly greater
improvements in students’ word-problem performance [18, 23].
Building on Polya’s work, Schoenfeld extended the framework by
incorporating metacognitive dimensions [58, 59]. Schoenfeld ar-
gued that successful problem-solving requires not only domain
knowledge and strategies but also metacognitive control—the abil-
ity to monitor one’s progress, evaluate the effectiveness of strategies,
and make adaptive decisions during the solving process. They high-
lighted that students often possess relevant knowledge but fail to
deploy it effectively due to poor metacognitive regulation.
Newman’s error analysis [49] provided another influential per-
spective by identifying specific types of errors students make at
different stages of problem-solving. Newman’s hierarchy distin-
guishes between reading errors, comprehension errors, transforma-
tion errors, process errors, and encoding errors. This framework
has been particularly useful for diagnostic purposes, as it allows
teachers to pinpoint where in the problem-solving process students
encounter difficulties [39, 56, 57]. Other researchers have proposed
variations and extensions of these frameworks. For example, Krulik
and Rudnick further specified the set of heuristics that are success-
ful at all levels of problem-solving [34]. Goulet-Lyle et al. highlight
the need to establish links between the explicit problem content
and students’ knowledge while devising a plan [22].
Despite the richness of these theoretical frameworks, contem-
porary mathematics education faces new challenges. Research has
highlighted that students struggle with the early stages of problem-
solving, particularly in comprehending textual information in word
problems, due to a growing tendency toward passive media con-
sumption [30, 61]. These evolving challenges highlight the need for
practical approaches that can guide and assess students’ problem-
solving processes.
2.2
Assessing and Supporting Problem-Solving
Processes: Challenges and Approaches
Assessing students’ problem-solving processes requires more than
evaluating their final answers. Researchers have argued that ef-
fective diagnosis must capture how students think and approach
across each stage of problem-solving [21, 29]. However, making
these cognitive processes visible has proven challenging, as much
of students’ reasoning remains implicit unless explicitly external-
ized [12, 26]. Several approaches have been developed to elicit
students’ thinking. Think-aloud protocols, where students verbal-
ize their reasoning while solving problems, have been widely used
in research settings [20, 40, 46]. However, these methods require
intensive teacher involvement and can interfere with students’ nat-
ural problem-solving flow, making them impractical for routine
classroom assessment [28]. Written explanations offer a more scal-
able alternative, yet the quality of students’ explanations is highly
sensitive to the design of prompts [8, 12, 54]. To address these
limitations, researchers have explored structured approaches, in-
cluding specialized problem tasks that inherently require students
to externalize specific aspects of their reasoning and analytical
frameworks to guide teachers’ evaluation of student work [32]. For
example, problem-specific scoring rubrics and carefully designed
sub-questions have been proposed to capture students’ reasoning
at each stage [16, 29, 31, 50].
Yet developing such materials for each problem demands substan-
tial expertise and time, making them difficult to scale in practice [31].
Therefore, we aim to explore a scalable approach leveraging LLMs
to generate problem-specific diagnostic materials while maintain-
ing pedagogical quality.
2.3
AI and LLMs in Mathematical Education
Intelligent tutoring systems (ITSs) have long supported step-level
diagnosis of mathematical problem solving. Cognitive tutors trace
each step of a student’s solution with a cognitive model and pro-
vide feedback when the student’s reasoning departs from a valid
solution path [4, 33]. Systems have also brought similar forms of
immediate feedback and formative assessment into classroom set-
tings [10, 25]. However, this level of diagnosis typically depends
on problems being modeled in advance. Even authoring tools in-
tended for non-programmers require substantial effort for each
problem [2, 42], while identifying which skills a problem assesses
still relies heavily on expert judgment [47]. This has largely limited
step-level diagnosis to pre-authored problem banks, rather than
the problems teachers already use or create for their classes.
Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have opened
new possibilities for supporting mathematics education at scale,
spanning question generation, automated scoring, and personalized
feedback. LLMs have demonstrated strong capabilities in generat-
ing mathematical problems and assessment items from natural lan-
guage specifications [5, 27, 44], with generated items approaching
human-authored quality on psychometric criteria [43], and recent
work has explored decomposing problems into sub-questions to
support deeper reasoning [6, 37, 60, 68]. However, most existing
approaches focus on generating standalone problems or factual
sub-questions, rather than diagnostic sub-questions aligned with
specific cognitive stages of problem-solving. In the domain of assess-
ment, LLMs have been applied to rubric generation and automated
scoring of open-ended responses [13, 14, 64, 65]. Scoring is most
reliable when the model is conditioned on an explicit rubric and
exemplar answers [13], yet rubrics are often predefined or designed
for evaluating complete solutions, rather than being generated adap-
tively to diagnose student competencies at each problem-solving
stage. Recent benchmarks evaluate whether LLM tutors can locate
the step at which a student’s solution goes wrong [41], but they

FORMATIVE STUDY: CHALLENGES IN
DIAGNOSING PROBLEM-SOLVING
PROCESSES IN PRACTICE
To better understand the practical challenges teachers face when
diagnosing students’ problem-solving skills, we conducted a forma-
tive study to examine whether stage-based questions are sufficient
for assessing students’ reasoning at each problem-solving stage.
Such questions are a natural starting point for scalable diagnosis,
as they can be applied across different problems without requiring
additional preparation.
3.1
Study Design
We worked with four mathematics teachers who teach students
in grades 3 through 6 (3 male, 1 female; mean age = 43.5 years;
mean teaching experience = 13.25 years). Drawing on the prior
work that decomposes the mathematical problem-solving process
into structured stages [30], we used four guiding questions—similar
to those in current diagnostic practice:
(1) Parse—Identifying key information: Underline the im-
portant information in the problem.
(2) Extract—Planning a solution strategy: Using the infor-
mation, describe your plan for solving the problem.
(3) Retrieve—Recalling relevant knowledge: Write down
the formulas or concepts needed to solve this problem.
(4) Combine—Executing the solution: Show your complete
solution process and arrive at the final answer.
The teachers then selected grade-appropriate math word problems
and presented them to students in a structured worksheet format
that incorporated these four questions.
A total of 48 students (distributed across grades 3-6) worked
through the three math word problems each, responding to all four
questions for each problem. The worksheet included separate re-
sponse spaces for each stage, allowing students to articulate their
reasoning step-by-step. After students completed their work, teach-
ers evaluated each student’s performance at each stage of problem
solving based on their written responses. Detailed information on
participant demographics and a sample worksheet are provided in
Appendix A.1.
3.2
Findings from Teacher Interviews
Following the assessment process, we conducted semi-structured in-
terviews with teachers to understand their experiences diagnosing
students’ problem-solving skills at each stage, based on their writ-
ten responses. Teachers consistently reported two major challenges
that limited the diagnostic value of this approach.
Limited Diagnostic Value of Student Responses. Teachers
reported that generic questions like “Describe your solution strat-
egy” frequently failed to surface students’ thought processes. One
teacher said, “The questions were the same regardless of the prob-
lem, so I couldn’t see how students were actually working through
it or where their thinking was breaking down.” Because the ques-
tions were not tailored to each specific problem, students’ responses
tended to be vague and disconnected from their actual problem-
solving process. Teachers noted that this issue was particularly
problematic in the planning and knowledge-recall stages, where
students struggled to externalize their reasoning, indicating that
generic questions were insufficient to elicit students’ strategy for-
mation and concept application reasoning.
Difficulty in Constructing Stage-Specific Rubrics. Teachers
found it difficult to develop clear, consistent evaluation criteria for
each problem-solving stage. Because each problem requires dif-
ferent reasoning at each stage, teachers struggled to define what
constituted adequate or exemplary responses. One teacher noted,
“I knew what kind of thinking I wanted to see, but it was hard to
articulate clear standards that would apply fairly across different
students’ responses.” This difficulty was compounded when evalu-
ating multiple problems, as teachers had to mentally adjust their
expectations for each problem’s context while trying to maintain
consistency in their judgments.
3.3
Implications
These findings highlight a fundamental tension in process-level
assessment: while problem-agnostic questions offer broad applica-
bility across problems, they fail to elicit the context-specific rea-
soning required to solve a given problem, including the problem’s
constraints, mathematical concepts, and solution strategies. Teach-
ers emphasized that effectively diagnosing problem-solving skills
would require questions and rubrics tailored to each problem’s
context—materials that make explicit what constitutes appropri-
ate reasoning at each stage for that particular problem. However,
they also acknowledged that creating such customized materials
for every problem would be time-consuming. Therefore, we ex-
plored how LLMs could generate problem-specific questions and
rubrics tailored to each problem-solving stage, which can serve as
diagnostic materials that teachers need but find too costly to create
manually.

STEP FRAMEWORK DEVELOPMENT
The findings of our formative study show that effective diagnosis
of students’ problem-solving processes requires diagnostic ques-
tions that make students’ reasoning explicit within the context
of a specific problem. However, supporting such context-aligned,
process-level diagnosis in a systematic and scalable manner first
requires a clear articulation of what skills should be diagnosed at

To address this need, we introduce STEP (STage-based Evaluation
of Problem-solving skills), a structured problem-solving diagnostic
framework to support process-level diagnosis of students’ math
word problem-solving. This framework provides a scaffold for gen-
erating context-aligned diagnostic materials, such as stage-specific
sub-questions and rubrics, that can be instantiated for individual
problems.
4.1
Theoretical Foundations
The development of STEP was informed by established problem-
solving frameworks in mathematics education, including Polya’s
four-stage model [52], Schoenfeld’s metacognitive framework [58,
59], Newman’s error analysis [49], and mathematics curriculum
standards [17]. While these frameworks provided conceptual ground-
ing for identifying problem-solving stages, they were developed
primarily for instructional purposes rather than diagnostic assess-
ment. Our goal was to operationalize these stages diagnostically,
focusing on what evidence teachers should look for and what ques-
tions can reliably elicit that evidence. This guided our subsequent
co-design process, in which theoretical stages were refined into
diagnosable skills.
4.2
Iterative Framework Development Through
Expert Interviews
Building on the four-stage structure used in the formative study
and grounded in the theoretical frameworks described above, we re-
fined the framework through multiple rounds of expert consultation.
We worked with six domain experts, one mathematics education
researcher with a Ph.D. in mathematics education, and five mathe-
matics teachers with over 10 years of teaching experience (3 male, 3
female; mean age = 42.3 years, SD = 7.67; mean teaching experience
= 16.2 years).
Interview Process. Through individual semi-structured inter-
views over multiple rounds, we collaboratively developed a more
refined framework for process-level diagnosis. For each session,
we prepared a shared document (Google Slides1) containing our
current framework draft and example sub-questions generated by
the LLM based on that framework. We asked experts to review
the materials and leave comments directly in that document. We
then conducted individual interviews to discuss their feedback in
depth. We specifically discussed (1) whether the current framework
captures skills that can be meaningfully diagnosed at each problem-
solving stage, (2) what common errors or misconceptions students
typically exhibit at each stage, and (3) what characteristics make
questions effective at eliciting diagnostic evidence for those skills.
Each interview was conducted via Zoom and lasted approximately
one hour.

Iterative Refinement. We began with the mathematics educa-
tion researcher, whose feedback laid the foundation for the frame-
work structure. The researcher emphasized the importance of dis-
tinguishing between general cognitive skills and problem-solving-
specific skills, guiding the decomposition of each stage into diagnos-
tically distinct sub-skills—ensuring that each targets a specific rea-
soning skill that can be independently assessed. We then conducted
sessions with the five mathematics teachers, whose classroom ex-
perience helped us refine the framework as a practical diagnostic
tool and improve the LLM pipeline for generating context-aligned
sub-questions. They emphasized that sub-questions should elicit
observable evidence of students’ thinking at each stage—for in-
stance, rather than simply asking students to perform a calculation,
questions should prompt students to articulate how they set up the
problem or why they chose a particular approach. This helped clar-
ify what counts as diagnostic evidence at each stage, and informed
iterative refinement to the framework and the sub-question gen-
eration pipeline. Across these five teacher sessions, we iteratively
refined the framework by clarifying stage boundaries, decompos-
ing stages into diagnostically assessable sub-skills, and adjusting
prompt-generation guidelines to better elicit students’ intermediate
reasoning. The study was approved by our university’s Institu-
tional Review Board (IRB). The mathematics education researcher
received KRW 100,000 in compensation, reflecting their role and
involvement in the early stages of framework construction, while
each mathematics teacher received KRW 50,000.
4.3
The Final STEP Framework
The resulting STEP framework consists of four problem-solving
stages and eight sub-skills (Figure 2). Rather than enumerating pro-
cedural steps, the framework is designed to support diagnosis by
clarifying what reasoning should be elicited and what constitutes di-
agnostic evidence at different points in the problem-solving process.
Although the problem-solving process is presented as a sequence of
stages, each stage and sub-skill is designed to be as diagnostically
independent as possible: each can be assessed based on the evidence
elicited by its corresponding question, without presupposing suc-
cessful performance in earlier stages. This design allows teachers
to pinpoint where breakdowns occur. A defining characteristic of
STEP is its emphasis on early stages of problem solving. Aligning
with prior work [30, 61], experts noted that today’s students often
struggle more with interpreting problem text and structuring infor-
mation than with calculations. Reflecting this reality, the framework
assigns equal diagnostic importance to early-stage skills. Together,
these elements provide a scaffold for generating context-aligned
diagnostic prompts and rubrics, which we operationalize through
the LLM-based pipeline described next.

5.1
Sub-Question Generation
This stage generates a set of diagnostic sub-questions that scaf-
fold students through the four problem-solving stages of the STEP
framework. The generation process consists of three modules: (1)
Problem Solver, which performs CoT-based problem solving, (2)
Sub-Questions Generator, which generates sub-questions based
on the problem-solving process, and (3) Verification and Refine-
ment, which iteratively refines the sub-questions through internal
verification.
This process begins by taking a math word problem, its final
answer, and the target grade level as input. When available, a model
solution or reference images can be optionally provided.
Problem Solver. (Figure 3- 1 ) Using this information, the pipeline
first reasons through the problem step by step, following a chain-
of-thought [66] process guided by the STEP framework as an inter-
pretive scaffold to identify the types of reasoning required at each
stage. The model also identifies the mathematical content domain
of the problem (e.g., Numbers and Operations, Patterns and Rela-
tionships, Geometry and Measurement, or Data and Possibility) [45].
This domain classification enables the pipeline to reference domain-
and grade-specific curriculum constraints that inform the design
of diagnostic sub-questions. For example, if a problem is catego-
rized within the Patterns and Relationships domain for grades 3–4,
the curriculum specifies that the problem should not involve com-
plex expressions such as mixed-operation formulas or general-term
representations when expressing patterns. In our current imple-
mentation, we operationalize these curriculum constraints using
Korea’s National Curriculum [45]. This choice reflects the scope of
our study—our dataset consists of Korean math word problems, and
our classroom study involves Korean elementary students. How-
ever, national curricula are formally specified across educational
systems worldwide [9]. Therefore, the pipeline can be adapted to
other countries by substituting the relevant curriculum standards.
Sub-Questions Generator. (Figure 3- 2 ) Based on the problem-
solving process and curriculum constraints, the pipeline trans-
forms the identified reasoning steps into a set of stage-specific
sub-questions aligned with the eight STEP sub-skills. Sub-questions
are generated under design constraints specified in the national
curriculum to preserve their diagnostic intent. Each sub-question
targets a single STEP sub-skill without combining multiple stages,
and avoids directive question prompts that specify calculations,
procedures, formulas, or expected answer forms.
Verification and Refinement. To improve the educational sound-
ness of generated sub-questions, the pipeline applies a multi-verifier
check [67]. Four verifiers assess complementary aspects of qual-
ity: stage elicitation (Figure 3- 3-1 ), context alignment (Figure 3-
3-2 ), prompt validity (Figure 3- 3-3 ), answer validity (Figure 3-
3-4 ). These verifiers operationalize the design constraints de-
scribed above by checking whether each sub-question satisfies
them in practice. The stage-elicitation verifier assesses whether
a sub-question targets the intended STEP sub-skill. The context-
alignment verifier evaluates whether a sub-question is grounded in
the target problem’s context. The prompt-validity verifier examines
whether a sub-question is appropriate for the target grade level and
curriculum scope. The answer-validity verifier checks whether the
exemplary answer to each sub-question is mathematically correct.
Each verifier scores a sub-question on a 0–5 scale. If any score falls
below the maximum-score threshold (5/5), the pipeline regenerates
the sub-question using verifier feedback, for up to three iterations.

5.2
Rubric Generation
This stage generates fine-grained rubrics for evaluating student
responses to each sub-question. Rather than relying on generic grad-
ing criteria, our pipeline grounds rubric design in diverse student
errors across performance levels.
Students’ Error Generator. (Figure 3- 4 ) For each of the eight
sub-questions, our pipeline generates student responses across
three performance levels—low, medium, and high. For both the
low and medium levels, three plausible incorrect responses are
generated, and for the high level, three correct-answer variations
are generated. For each sub-question, the prompt incorporates the
correct responses from prior steps, thereby preventing cascading er-
rors from propagating to later stages. The prompts also incorporate
skill-specific error characteristics grounded in educational research
(e.g., Newman’s error types [49]), such that low-level errors reflect
fundamental misunderstandings while medium-level errors reflect
partial comprehension with minor gaps. Additionally, grade-level
achievement standards and knowledge scope data are incorporated
to ensure that generated responses remain grade-appropriate.
Error Pattern Analyzer. (Figure 3- 5 ) All nine simulated re-
sponses (three per level) for a given sub-question are analyzed
to identify qualitative differences across levels. This analysis ex-
tracts distinct, non-overlapping error patterns and establishes clear
boundaries between low, medium, and high performance.
Rubric Generator. (Figure 3- 6 ) Using the error pattern anal-
ysis, the pipeline constructs a three-tier grading rubric—High (cor-
rect), Mid (partial understanding), and Low (fundamental error)—
with criteria and representative examples drawn directly from the
simulated responses. This approach ensures that rubric criteria are
specific and actionable, being grounded in concrete error patterns
observed by the Error Pattern Analyzer.
All modules in the pipeline employ OpenAI’s GPT-4o model to
leverage its multimodal capabilities for processing problems that
may include diagrams or visual elements. Detailed prompts for each
module are provided in the supplementary materials.

TWO-STAGE EVALUATION
To examine how our STEP framework and LLM pipeline function
in practice, we conducted a two-stage evaluation. Stage 1 involved
an expert review to gather feedback on the mathematical and ped-
agogical validity of the diagnostic materials generated by the LLM
pipeline across diverse problems. Based on the expert feedback,
we refined the pipeline and developed STEPwise, an end-to-end
teacher-in-the-loop system that supports the workflow from di-
agnostic material generation to student diagnosis. Stage 2 then
involved a month-long classroom deployment of STEPwise with 5
teachers and 64 students, examining how the STEP framework and
STEPwise system function in real educational contexts to support
process-level diagnosis of students’ problem-solving.
6.1
Stage 1: Expert Review
We conducted an expert review with mathematics teachers to gather
structured feedback on diagnostic materials generated by the LLM
pipeline based on STEP. The review aimed to (1) understand how
teachers interpret the pedagogical appropriateness of the generated
sub-questions and rubrics, and (2) identify practical considerations
and refinements for classroom use.
6.1.1
Review Protocol. Dataset. We used math problem data from
the AI Hub [1], which provides curriculum-aligned problems with
problem statements, correct answers, model solutions, and difficulty
labels across grade levels. We filtered for medium-to-high difficulty
math word problems at grades 3–6 that require multi-step reasoning,
and sampled 36 problems covering diverse mathematical content

domains. Due to the smaller pool of eligible lower-grade problems,
we combined grades 3 and 4 and sampled 12 problems for this band,
and 12 problems each for grades 5 and 6. Details on the sampled
problems are in Appendix (Table 4).
Participants. Six mathematics teachers with at least 5 years of
teaching experience were recruited through open calls and snow-
ball sampling (3 male, 3 female; mean age = 38.2, mean teaching
experience = 11.93 years), with two assigned to each grade band
(grades 3–4, 5, and 6). Each teacher evaluated 12 problems, each
consisting of eight stages, resulting in 96 sub-questions and their
accompanying rubrics. Details are in Appendix (Table 5).
Procedure. The review consisted of three phases: (1) a 30-minute
orientation introducing the research purpose, STEP framework,
and evaluation criteria, (2) an asynchronous evaluation session
of approximately 2.5 hours in which teachers assessed the gener-
ated diagnostic materials using given metrics, and (3) a one-hour
follow-up interview to discuss evaluation reasoning and suggest
improvements for practical classroom use. Each participant was
compensated 160,000 KRW for their time.
Review Dimensions. Drawing on prior work for assessing the
validity and usability of educational assessment materials [11, 53],
we defined structured review dimensions to guide teacher feedback.
Teachers reviewed the generated materials at two levels of gran-
ularity: each of the eight sub-skills individually, and the full set
generated for a given problem. At the sub-skill level, teachers re-
viewed the materials generated for each of the eight STEP sub-skills
on three dimensions: whether the sub-question and rubric target
the intended sub-skill for diagnosis (Construct Validity), whether
their complexity, information load, and phrasing suit the target
grade level (Level Appropriateness), and whether the exemplary
answer and rubric criteria are mathematically correct (Mathemati-
cal Accuracy). At the problem level, teachers reviewed the full set
of materials generated for a given problem, examining how the
STEP framework was concretely instantiated in a specific problem-
solving context. They judged whether the materials naturally con-
nect to the problem context and guide students through the solving
process coherently (Problem-Context Alignment), and whether
they sufficiently cover the full problem-solving process required
by the problem (Comprehensiveness). For all dimensions, teachers
made binary (Yes/No) judgments alongside free-form comments,
encouraging clear decisions while capturing the reasoning behind
them and surfacing disagreements for follow-up. The exact review
questions are in Appendix A.3.1.
Supplementary Review. For each grade band, two teachers
evaluated the same set of problems. When they disagreed, two
additional mathematics teachers who had not participated in the
primary review served as third-party tiebreakers, each assigned
half of the conflicting cases, to provide supplementary pedagogical
perspectives and reach a final determination.

6.2.3
Classroom Study Results.
Scalability of Material Generation and Diagnosis. Over the
month, the 5 teachers prepared diagnostic materials for 90 problems
in total, amounting to 720 sub-questions and accompanying rubrics,
and reported no notable difficulty in using the system (mean SUS
score [7] of 83.5). As teachers chose the problems themselves, the
materials reflected what each class was actually working on, and
varied in topic and difficulty—ranging from regular curriculum
problems to competition-level ones. Teachers attributed both the
volume and this freedom of choice to the fact that the only input
required was a problem they already intended to use: the system
produced the stage-specific sub-questions and rubrics for what-
ever they brought to it, and scored student responses against them
once collected. Teachers described manually authoring eight sub-
questions and corresponding three-tier rubrics for every problem
as impractical alongside their regular teaching. They valued being
able to generate diagnostic materials for any problem they were
already using in their classes. They therefore viewed STEPwise
as enabling a form of process-level diagnosis that they could not
otherwise sustain. CT4 noted that, among the systems she had used,
its questions were the closest to how questions are posed in her
own classroom.
Making the Problem-Solving Process Visible. Teachers con-
sistently described the central benefit of STEPwise as observing
the flow of student thinking rather than the correctness of a final
answer. The stage-based decomposition let them check students’
comprehension, conceptual knowledge, and logical reasoning sepa-
rately, and identify how far a student had progressed and where
their reasoning broke down. In practice, when students cannot solve
a problem, they often simply say “I don’t know,” and the teacher
must probe through follow-up questions to locate the breakdown;
STEPwise made this visible without additional diagnostic ques-
tioning. Teachers also valued the three-tier rubrics as an explicit
marking standard: having High, Medium, and Low criteria spelled
out gave them a clear reference point when giving written feedback
on student work. This visibility changed what teachers knew about
their own students. CT5 reported being surprised that a student
they had vaguely assumed was doing well turned out to be stuck at
a particular stage, and that seeing this made it much clearer what
support to provide for that student. Teachers also noted that the
student diagnosis reports let them identify which students were
struggling at a specific stage, such as problem comprehension. CT2
and CT5 further pointed out that reports of this kind are directly
usable in parent consultations, since they let a teacher explain con-
cretely where a student struggled rather than simply saying that
the student is doing well or poorly overall.
Student Experience. Students rated the approach moderately
positively across all four survey dimensions (means between 3.59
and 3.89 out of 5; see Figure 5), with distributions skewed toward
agreement alongside a persistent minority who disagreed. The
highest-rated dimension was self-awareness of their strengths and
weaknesses (𝑀= 3.89,𝑆𝐷= 1.04), suggesting that working through
the stages made students’ own reasoning gaps visible to them. This
was followed by willingness to use the approach for future learning
(𝑀= 3.72, 𝑆𝐷= 1.20), perceived usefulness of the step-by-step
structure (𝑀= 3.63, 𝑆𝐷= 1.20), and ease of expressing their rea-
soning process (𝑀= 3.59, 𝑆𝐷= 1.12)—the lowest-rated dimension,
consistent with teachers’ observation that articulation was the
central difficulty. In open-ended responses, students reported that
breaking problems into stages helped them solve more carefully
and write out their thinking more explicitly than usual, and that
using what they had established in earlier stages to answer later
ones helped them see the structure of the problem. At the same
time, several reported that writing out each step took more time
than usual, since they needed to explain their thinking instead of
just giving an answer.
How Students Engaged with Stage-Based Sub-Questions. A
finding teachers repeatedly described as unexpected was that de-
composing a problem into stages did not make it easier for students.
Teachers had assumed that presenting a problem step by step would
lower its difficulty, but many students found the stage-based sub-
questions harder than the original problem. The difficulty lay not in

the mathematics but in the act of articulation: breaking one’s think-
ing into steps and expressing it in sentences or expressions was
itself the obstacle. Even students who ordinarily solve such prob-
lems well became stuck, and students who had never written out
explanations sometimes gave up partway. Teachers observed that
students across the board tried to jump directly to an answer rather
than dividing their thinking into parts. Grade-level differences were
pronounced. CT2 contrasted her elementary students in grades 3–4,
who tried to write down what they knew in as much detail as they
could even on competition-level problems, with her middle-school
students, who more often judged a problem to be difficult from
the outset and resisted recording their reasoning one stage at a
time. Teachers read this as evidence that differences in disposition
and in the ability to express one’s thinking were more visible than
differences in mathematical ability, and concluded that these are
themselves things to be taught rather than prerequisites to be as-
sumed. This asymmetry echoes prior deployments of LLM-based
scaffolding in K–12 classrooms, where a uniform step-by-step struc-
ture benefited students overall while placing a disproportionate
burden on those with lower proficiency [48].
Even so, teachers observed changes in how students engaged
with difficult problems. Although the level of detail in students’
written solutions did not change dramatically, students spent longer
thinking before writing, and when they encountered a hard problem,
they re-read it and considered alternative approaches instead of
giving up. CT1–CT4 also noticed a change in how students asked for
help: rather than asking for the answer, some students began asking
questions such as “which part should I think about?” or requesting
a small hint, and students developed the habit of first grasping
what a question was asking before writing their solution. Teachers
therefore characterized the sub-questions as not only a diagnostic
instrument but a learning experience in themselves, giving students
practice in working through a single problem thoroughly.
Adapting the Decomposition to Problems and Students. Teach-
ers regarded stage-based decomposition as pedagogically sound
overall: dividing a problem into stages let them see how far a stu-
dent’s understanding extended, and the stage-specific questions
themselves gave students a direction for what to consider next.
They saw it as particularly valuable for students who struggle with
mathematics or with sustained thinking, since it replaces the blan-
ket judgment that a student “cannot solve problems” with a specific
stage at which difficulty arises. Within this, they pointed to cases
where the granularity could flex. CT1 noted that for some problems
the intermediate stages already revealed enough about a student’s
reasoning, so the full eight stages were not always necessary. Teach-
ers also observed that the students who stood to benefit most were
sometimes the ones who found eight separate sub-questions most
burdensome, and suggested merging adjacent stages into fewer,
broader sub-questions for them. For students with strong or cre-
ative reasoning, they cautioned that a fixed sequence of stages can
channel thinking along the provided path rather than letting stu-
dents devise their own. Teachers also saw room for sub-questions
that extend thinking rather than lead to the next answer. CT1 ob-
served that some sub-questions read as guiding students toward
the following step, and wanted more questions asking why stu-
dents thought as they did, what alternative solutions exist, or how
the situation would change if a condition were altered. A paral-
lel point applied to the rubrics: teachers found the criteria clearly
differentiated across score levels, but CT2 felt they were oriented
toward stage-wise correctness and wanted criteria that could also
credit diverse or creative solution paths. Finally, teachers found
the diagnostic reports useful for grasping a student’s overall state,
while CT2 and CT4 wanted them to be more specific and more
actionable—which types of errors recurred and offering guidance
for future instruction.
7
DISCUSSION
We discuss the broader implications of our findings across three
dimensions: students’ self-diagnosis, personalized learning, and
scaling toward automated deployment. We also reflect on the limi-
tations of the current work and directions for future research.
7.1
Supporting Student Self-Diagnosis
Although STEPwise was originally designed to support teach-
ers, our classroom study suggests its potential to support student
self-diagnosis as well. Self-awareness of their own strengths and
weaknesses was the highest-rated dimension in the student sur-
vey (𝑀= 3.89), and several students noted that the stage-based
structure helped them articulate their thinking more explicitly than
usual. Teachers likewise observed that students began re-reading
problems, considering alternative approaches, and asking which
part to think about rather than asking for the answer. This points to
a broader opportunity: because each sub-question targets a diagnos-
tically distinct sub-skill within the STEP framework, stage-based
sub-questions may naturally scaffold metacognitive reflection [19],
helping students recognize precisely where their reasoning breaks
down without requiring explicit teacher intervention. This aligns
with research on self-regulated learning, which emphasizes the
importance of students monitoring and evaluating their own cog-
nitive processes as a foundation for deeper learning [69]. In such a
setting, STEPwise could present stage-specific sub-questions while
withholding the full solution, allowing students to work through
each reasoning stage and develop awareness of where their think-
ing begins to falter. Future work could explore designs that provide
students with immediate feedback at each stage, enabling them to
self-diagnose their reasoning gaps and take greater ownership of
their learning process.
7.2
From Diagnosis to Personalized Learning
Our findings open a broader opportunity beyond diagnosis: leverag-
ing stage-level reasoning analysis to support personalized learning.
Rather than treating diagnostic results as static assessments, stage-
specific performance profiles could inform targeted instructional
interventions tailored to each student’s reasoning gaps. For in-
stance, a student who possesses strong problem comprehension
skills could skip that stage ahead, while a student who struggles
with information structuring could receive additional scaffolded
practice on organizing given conditions into variables or tables.
Such an adaptive approach would move beyond one-size-fits-all
diagnosis toward more personalized support, and connect naturally
to the broader literature on mastery-based and adaptive learning
systems [63].

Conference’17, July 2017, Washington, DC, USA
Yoonsu Kim et al.
Our classroom study points to a further dimension of person-
alization: not only which stage to support, but how finely to de-
compose it. Teachers judged stage-based diagnosis most valuable
for students who struggle, yet those students found eight separate
sub-questions the most burdensome, and teachers suggested merg-
ing adjacent stages for them. Prior classroom deployments report a
similar asymmetry, where step-by-step LLM scaffolding improved
students’ output overall yet demotivated lower-proficiency students
and increased their reliance on the system [48]. At the other end,
teachers cautioned that a fixed sequence of stages may channel
students with strong or creative reasoning along the provided path
rather than letting them devise their own. Granularity is there-
fore not a fixed property of the framework but a parameter to be
set per student and per problem: stage-level profiles could inform
how many sub-questions a student receives, and which stages are
merged, in addition to what content to reteach. Future work could
explore how to translate stage-level diagnostic results into concrete
personalized instructional interventions, and how much decompo-
sition each student needs to make their reasoning visible without
overwhelming them.
7.3
Toward Scalable and Automated Diagnosis
STEPwise currently relies on a teacher-in-the-loop structure in
which teachers review and refine generated materials before deploy-
ment. This design ensures reliability, and teachers in our classroom
study did not find the review process burdensome. Still, scaling this
approach to broader contexts, such as large classrooms or online
learning platforms, would benefit from further reducing teacher
overhead. In our current implementation, STEPwise already takes
a step in this direction: rubrics generated by the pipeline are used
directly by an LLM to automatically score student responses, and
the resulting scores are aggregated into a diagnostic report that
summarizes each student’s performance across reasoning stages.
This end-to-end flow—from problem input to scored diagnostic
report—demonstrates the feasibility of automating the diagnosis
process with minimal teacher involvement.
Further scaling involves two bottlenecks. First, rubric quality:
teachers in our expert review noted that rubric criteria occasionally
narrowed to specific cases, which may reflect limited variability in
the responses simulating students’ errors. Simply increasing the
number of simulations would add computational overhead without
guaranteeing broader coverage. A more promising direction is to in-
corporate real student data as it accumulates through deployment—
abstracting recurring error patterns and reasoning strategies across
problems and stages—and using these empirically grounded pat-
terns to inform the simulation process itself. This would enable
the pipeline to generate richer, more representative rubrics even
for new problems, improving diagnostic accuracy without requir-
ing per-problem data collection. Second, response collection: in our
classroom study, students completed worksheets on paper, which
allowed them to express their reasoning naturally. To make this
compatible with digital scoring, STEPwise allowed teachers to up-
load photos of these paper worksheets for automatic recognition.
However, this workaround does not fully resolve the challenge
of scaling response collection, as uploading and managing files
for a much larger number of students would itself become a bur-
den for teachers. Therefore, future work should explore computer-
based input formats that preserve this expressiveness—for example,
adaptive block-based interfaces that present relevant mathematical
operators without prescribing the sequence of operations.
Automating diagnosis also raises the question of what a diagnos-
tic report should contain. Teachers in our classroom study found
the reports useful for grasping a student’s overall state, but wanted
them to be more specific and more actionable: which types of er-
rors recurred across problems and what concepts or problem types
to teach next. They also wanted results to accumulate, so that re-
peated diagnoses would show how a student’s reasoning changes
over time rather than reporting each worksheet in isolation. Both
point beyond per-worksheet scoring toward a longitudinal record
of stage-level performance, which would also supply exactly the
empirically grounded error patterns described above.
7.4
Limitations and Future Work
This work has several limitations that point to directions for future
research. First, stage-based sub-questions do not merely observe
the problem-solving process—they structure it. What our approach
diagnoses is therefore a student’s reasoning under a given decom-
position, which may differ from how the same student would ap-
proach the problem unaided. Teachers’ concern that a fixed stage
sequence may channel students with strong reasoning along the
provided path is one manifestation of this, and future work should
examine how the presence and granularity of sub-questions shape
the very process being diagnosed. Second, while our study cap-
tured students’ immediate experience with the diagnostic materials,
we did not measure whether engagement with stage-based sub-
questions leads to gains in problem-solving performance, nor did
we follow the instructional decisions teachers made based on the
accumulated diagnostic reports. Longitudinal studies that trace
this full loop—from diagnosis to targeted instruction to subsequent
performance—would be an important next step in establishing the
educational efficacy of this approach. Third, STEPwise’s diagnosis
step supports the process-level assessment workflow, but we did
not treat it as a fully validated diagnostic instrument in this work:
we did not systematically compare STEPwise’s scores against teach-
ers’ own scoring of the same responses, an important next step for
establishing diagnostic accuracy at scale. Still, teachers raised no
concerns about the correctness of the generated reports over the
course of the deployment. Together, these directions would help es-
tablish the broader educational value of LLM-generated diagnostic
materials as a scalable tool for supporting process-level assessment
in mathematics education.
8
CONCLUSION
We investigated how fine-grained diagnosis of students’ mathemat-
ical problem-solving processes can be structured and supported
in classroom practice. Our formative study identified two practi-
cal challenges: generic stage-based questions often failed to elicit
problem-specific reasoning, and teachers lacked clear criteria for
assessing students’ responses consistently. To address these chal-
lenges, we developed STEP, which refines four sequential problem-
solving stages into eight diagnostic sub-skills, and an LLM-based

pipeline that generates problem-specific sub-questions and rubrics.
An expert review found strong construct validity and mathematical
accuracy while revealing limitations in grade-level and problem-
context alignment, motivating the teacher-in-the-loop design of
STEPwise. In a month-long classroom deployment, teachers re-
ported that students’ stage-by-stage responses helped them locate
reasoning breakdowns, while students generally valued the sup-
port but varied in their ability to articulate each stage. Together,
these findings identify both opportunities and design requirements
for making fine-grained, process-level diagnosis more practical at
classroom scale.
